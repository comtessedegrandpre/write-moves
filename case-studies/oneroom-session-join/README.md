# OneRoom Session Join: From Concept to UX Copy

The Product team was redesigning its session-join experience to reduce the number of steps required for participants to enter a OneRoom session.

As the interaction model took shape, I contributed to the UX writing for the new experience, working alongside the product strategist and product designer. I proposed the voice direction for the experience, and developed and refined copy for screens and states throughout the flow.

My focus was on making the experience clear, welcoming, and consistent with OneRoom's positioning across both meeting and learning contexts.

## From rough concept to product experience

The Product team began by sketching a few early concepts for the experience. These were intentionally rough. The goal was to explore the content, messaging, and sequence of information before the screens were developed into polished UI.

The sketches explored several participant states, including first-time sign-in, an error state, and joining a session through a link without signing in.

The product strategist and product designer then expanded the initial concepts into a more complete set of screens and interactions. I continued contributing and refining the copy as the designs evolved, working with the team to shape the content for the different states a participant might encounter.

The resulting flow included:

* Sign in
* Page not found
* Access denied
* Link expired
* SSO sign-out confirmation
* Join screen
* Waiting room

## Establishing the voice

Before developing the individual screen copy, I proposed a voice direction for the experience: *global, professional-yet-accessible, blending the learning and meeting narratives.*

The sign-in screen was an opportunity to put that direction into practice and introduce OneRoom's broader product experience before the participant even entered a session.

### Early concept

> **Collaboration inspires growth.**
> A space to meet, learn, and collaborate, wherever you are.

The first line provided an aspirational element, while the second grounded the message in what OneRoom actually enabled.

Importantly, this copy made it from the initial concept into the production product.

The wording also reflected the breadth of OneRoom. It was not exclusively a meeting product. The experience needed to work equally naturally in learning, training, and collaborative contexts.

## Writing for different states

Not every screen needed the same degree of personality.

For example, my initial concept for the page-not-found state used a more human, branded approach:

> **The best journeys can take a few wrong turns.**
> Good thing you're not traveling alone. Let's guide you back.

**Take me Home**

The production experience ultimately used the more direct **Page not found** treatment.

That evolution illustrates an important part of UX writing: a consistent voice does not mean every message needs to be expressive. For an error state, immediate recognition of what happened can be more useful than a branded message.

The content evolved alongside the product rather than being treated as fixed copy from the beginning.

## Asking for just enough information

The guest join experience needed to collect a small amount of information from participants who were not signing in through SSO.

### Step 1: Identify the participant

> **Welcome! Get ready to join the session**
> Enter your work or school email

**Continue**

The introductory message established a welcoming tone while the field itself remained straightforward about what was required.

### Step 2: Let participants choose how they are identified

> **Enter your preferred name**
> Share a bit about yourself *(optional)*

The use of **preferred name** was intentional. It allowed participants to enter the name they wanted to be known by during the session, including a shorter form or nickname when their full name was lengthy.

The optional introduction field gave participants another way to provide context about themselves. They could enter information such as a title, location, or pronouns, but they were not required to disclose anything beyond their name.

## Designing content for the whole experience

The "Share a bit about yourself" field also had a constraint that was not immediately visible on the screen.

The information entered there would appear on the participant's name tag on the OneRoom video wall. The character limit therefore served a practical purpose: it kept the information short enough to remain useful and legible in the physical room.

This was an example of considering content in the context of the broader product experience, rather than treating the interface as an isolated screen.

The copy and field constraints worked together:

| Content decision     | Purpose                                                    |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Work or school email | Identify the participant                                   |
| Preferred name       | Let participants control how they are identified           |
| About yourself       | Give other participants useful context                     |
| Optional field       | Avoid requiring unnecessary personal information           |
| Character limit      | Keep the information appropriate for the physical name tag |

The interface was digital, but the content ultimately became part of a physical OneRoom experience.

## Content across the join flow

The final experience included a range of situations beyond the normal sign-in path, including access problems, expired links, SSO sign-out, and the waiting room.

I contributed copy across these different states, working with the team to keep the experience coherent while ensuring that participants had the information they needed at each step.

The work was less about finding a clever phrase for each screen and more about considering what the participant needed to know, what they needed to do next, and how each message fit into the larger OneRoom experience.

## From concept to production

This project is a useful example of how my UX writing work developed collaboratively alongside product and design.

I started with rough content and interaction concepts, the product designer developed the broader visual and interaction design, and I continued to contribute and refine copy as the experience evolved. The product strategist also contributed meaningful content as part of the collaborative process.

Some of the original language carried directly into the production product. Other content became more concise as the team refined the experience.

That progression is typical of product work: the first idea is a starting point, not necessarily the final answer.
